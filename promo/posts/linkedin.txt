In August 2025, at TinkerHub's Useless Projects 2.0 hackathon in Kerala, my teammate and I built something gloriously pointless: SlowLang, an esoteric programming language with a speed limit.

The premise is simple. SlowLang enforces a 30 WPM typing cap. Type faster than the limit and the ASCII turtle mascot rage-locks you out for two seconds, then mocks you in haiku. Type too slowly and it calls you lazy. There is a narrow band of acceptable mediocrity, and the IDE polices it.

Why build this? Because hackathons like Useless Projects reward exactly one thing: commitment to a bad idea executed well. We implemented a real lexer, a Tkinter desktop IDE, a sarcasm engine with a lazy-refusal probability roll, and a zero-install browser demo that plays by identical rules. The language is Python-adjacent, technically functional, and philosophically hostile.

It won first place.

A few honest lessons from the build:

1. Constraints are a design tool. The WPM cap sounds like a joke but it forced real decisions about event timing, state machines, and forgiveness windows.

2. Humor needs engineering rigor. Every joke line ships in two places (desktop and web demo), tested, with the same thresholds. Comedy that breaks isn't comedy, it's a bug.

3. Ship the demo. Every feature exists in a playable browser build. Nobody downloads a joke.
